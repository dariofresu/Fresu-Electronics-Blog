Redefining Current and Charge: A Field-Based Perspective on Electromagnetism

Classical electromagnetism defines current as the flow of charge through a conductor — carried by electrons drifting at roughly 73 µm/s. But signals propagate through a PCB at 150 million m/s. Something doesn't add up. A field-based reframing resolves the contradiction.

Dario Fresu Principal EMC Architect · Fresu Electronics April 20, 2025 · 11 min read

Classical electromagnetism defines current and charge as foundational concepts. Current represents the flow of electric charge, typically carried by electrons in conductors, while charge is an intrinsic property of particles such as electrons and protons, serving as the source of electromagnetic fields.

However, this conventional understanding reveals significant limitations — particularly when you look at the speed at which electromagnetic effects propagate compared to how slowly charge carriers actually move. This article introduces a perspective that reframes the classical definitions, emphasising the primacy of electromagnetic fields over particle motion. Not new equations. Not alternative physics. A conceptual shift that makes circuits easier to reason about.

Left: conventional view — electricity as electrons moving inside conductors. Right: the corrected view — electricity as electromagnetic fields propagating in the space between conductors.

The Classical View: Current and Charge

In classical electromagnetism, current is defined as the rate of flow of electric charge:

For a copper wire with a 1 mm² cross-section carrying 1 A, with an electron density of approximately 8.5×10²⁸ m⁻³, the drift velocity works out to roughly 73.4 µm/s — comparable to the speed of a snail.

Charge as a Fundamental Property

Classically, charge is an inherent characteristic of particles: electrons carry −1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C, protons +1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C. Charges generate electric fields as described by Gauss's law, and moving charges produce magnetic fields as described by Ampère's law. This framework positions charges as the primary sources of fields, with their motion driving electromagnetic phenomena.

Where the Classical View Breaks Down

Speed discrepancy

Electrons drift at ~10 µm/s. Yet a light turns on in 6.67 ns across a 1-metre circuit. The electromagnetic wave propagates through the dielectric between conductors at 1/√(µ₀ ε₀ εr) — approximately 150 million m/s for typical PCB materials (εr ≈ 4). Signal propagation is billions of times faster than electron drift. If electrons were carrying the energy, this would be impossible.

Signal propagation in a PCB. The electromagnetic wave travels through the dielectric at a fraction of the speed of light — far faster than electrons can move.

Energy propagation

The traditional view says electrons carry energy along the wire. But energy actually propagates in the space surrounding the conductor, as described by the Poynting vector S = E × H. In a PCB trace or coaxial cable, the electric field is perpendicular to the conductors; the Poynting vector points along the direction of signal travel — in the dielectric, not inside the copper.

Electromagnetic fields around a transmission line. The electric field (E) is perpendicular to the conductors; the magnetic field (H) forms circles around them; the Poynting vector (S) points in the direction of propagation — in the space between conductors, not inside them.

A Field-Based Perspective

To address these limitations, a reframing centres on the primacy of electromagnetic fields.

New definition — current

Current is the manifestation of electromagnetic field energy flux in a conductor — a measurable flow of charge resulting from the response of charge carriers to the induced electric field. The primary driver is the propagation of electromagnetic fields in the space around the conductor, delivering energy via the Poynting vector and inducing currents through local field interactions.

Mechanism 02

Induced Electric Fields Drive Current

The wave propagating in the dielectric induces electric fields at the conductor surfaces, which drive charge carriers. The current density J is proportional to the field strength, scaled by the material's conductivity σ: J = σE. In the signal conductor, the field drives current in the direction of wave propagation; in the return conductor, the field is oriented oppositely, producing a return current of equal magnitude.

Mechanism 04

Charge Motion is Secondary

Electrons respond to the induced electric field, but their slow drift velocity (~10 µm/s) does not drive the rapid effects seen in circuits. The fields, propagating at the speed determined by the medium, are the primary mechanism.

Redefining Charge: Beyond Particles

If current is a field-induced effect, and electrons are not the primary entities establishing fields, then the nature of charge itself needs reconsidering.

The classical view runs into two problems. First, electromagnetic waves propagate in a vacuum without local charges — fields can exist independently. Second, if charges create fields and fields act on charges, what is the ultimate source? A field-based view of current suggests fields may be more fundamental.

There is also a deeper tension in the classical framework: free space is defined as a vacuum — an emptiness devoid of any substance — yet it has permittivity (ε₀) and permeability (µ₀) that enable electromagnetic propagation. How can complete emptiness possess properties? This contradiction suggests free space is not truly empty, but a medium with intrinsic dynamics that supports field propagation.

New definition — charge

Charge is a localized distortion or concentration of electromagnetic field energy within the free space medium, sustained by the medium's permittivity and permeability. What is conventionally called "charge" — an electron's −1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C — is an emergent phenomenon: a stable, resonant pattern of electric and magnetic fields, not an intrinsic property of a particle.

Circuit Dynamics

Displacement current closes the current loop at the wavefront as the signal propagates — establishing opposite currents in the signal and return paths at wave speed.

In a circuit with a signal line and return path, the electromagnetic wave propagates in the dielectric between the conductors. The electric field, perpendicular to the conductors, induces longitudinal fields inside them that drive currents in opposite directions. Displacement current closes the loop at the wavefront — not through electron drift, but through field dynamics propagating at a fraction of the speed of light.

Conclusion

Reinterpreting current and charge through a field-based framework offers a more internally consistent understanding of electromagnetism. Current becomes a field-induced effect — driven by energy flux — with electron motion as a secondary response. Charge becomes a localized distortion of field energy within the free space medium, not an intrinsic particle property.

This resolves the long-standing inconsistency between the slow drift of electrons and the near-light-speed propagation of signals. More importantly, it reframes the mental model: fields are the primary actors. Conductors guide them. Currents are their response.

For PCB and hardware engineers, this is not just theoretical — it changes how you think about return paths, signal integrity, and EMI. The field exists in the dielectric. The return current mirrors the signal current because the wave requires it. That's not intuition. That's the physics.

Stop designing for electron flow. Design for the field.
